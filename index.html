The Geometry of the Gauss Map

How does a surface bend in each tangent direction, and how do we compute that bending?

The first fundamental form $I_p$ measures intrinsic lengths, angles, and areas—yet a flat sheet and a rolled cylinder share identical $I_p$ while bending completely differently in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Chapter 3 captures extrinsic bending by tracking how the unit surface normal $N: S \to S^2$ tilts across the tangent plane.

Section I · Normal Curvature & Meusnier's Theorem

The Gauss map turns surface bending into a linear operator on the tangent plane

Fix a differentiable unit normal $N$ on an oriented neighborhood of $S$. For any unit-speed curve $\alpha(s)$ on $S$ passing through $\alpha(0)=p$ with velocity $\alpha'(0)=v$, the acceleration $\alpha''(0)$ can split into turning inside $T_pS$ and bending out of $T_pS$. The normal curvature isolates only the normal component: $k_n = \langle \alpha''(0), N(p) \rangle = k\cos\theta$.

Differentiating $\langle \alpha'(s), N(\alpha(s))\rangle = 0$ yields $\langle \alpha'', N\rangle = -\langle \alpha', dN(\alpha')\rangle$, so $k_n(v) = -\langle dN_p(v), v\rangle$. By Meusnier’s Theorem, every surface curve through $p$ sharing the same tangent line has the exact same normal curvature—only the direction $v$ matters.

The Gauss Map & Its Differential

$$N: S \longrightarrow S^2, \qquad dN_p(v) = \left.\frac{d}{dt} N(\alpha(t))\right|_{t=0} \in T_{N(p)}S^2 \cong T_p S$$

Because $|N|=1$, $dN_p(v) \perp N(p)$, letting us identify $T_{N(p)}S^2$ with $T_p S$ as parallel planes.

Normal Curvature & Cylinder Example

$$k_n(v) = -\langle dN_p(v), v \rangle, \qquad k_n^{\text{cyl}}(\theta) = -\frac{\cos^2\theta}{R}$$

On a cylinder with outward normal, the circular cross-section ($\theta=0$) gives $k_n = -1/R$ while the axial line ($\theta=\pi/2$) gives $k_n = 0$.

Section II · Shape Operator, Euler's Formula & Dupin Indicatrix

Every normal curvature sits between $k_1$ and $k_2$, traced cleanly by a conic in $T_p S$

Because $dN_p$ is self-adjoint ($\langle dN_p(v), w\rangle = \langle v, dN_p(w)\rangle$), the shape operator $A_p = -dN_p$ admits an orthonormal basis $\{e_1, e_2\}$ of principal directions with eigenvalues $k_1 \ge k_2$ (the principal curvatures). For any unit vector $v = e_1\cos\theta + e_2\sin\theta$, the second fundamental form $II_p(v) = \langle A_p(v), v\rangle$ simplifies to Euler’s Formula.

Visualizing $II_p(x e_1 + y e_2) = k_1 x^2 + k_2 y^2 = \pm 1$ in the tangent plane produces the Dupin indicatrix: a conic whose radial distance in direction $\theta$ is $r(\theta) = 1/\sqrt{|k_n(\theta)|}$—an ellipse when $K>0$, conjugate hyperbolas revealing the two asymptotic directions ($k_n=0$) when $K<0$, and parallel lines when $K=0$.

Second Form & Euler's Formula

$$II_p(w) = \langle A_p(w), w \rangle, \qquad k_n(\theta) = k_1\cos^2\theta + k_2\sin^2\theta$$

Maximum and minimum normal bending occur along the orthogonal principal axes $e_1$ and $e_2$.

Dupin Indicatrix & Rodrigues

$$k_1 x^2 + k_2 y^2 = \pm 1, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}N(\alpha(t)) = -k(t)\alpha'(t)$$

Lines of curvature satisfy Olinde Rodrigues’ criterion: $dN(\alpha')$ is collinear with $\alpha'$.

Section III · Gaussian & Mean Curvature in Local Coordinates

Six canonical surfaces show how $K$, $H$, and umbilical points organize local geometry

Given any parametrization $\mathbf{x}(u,v)$, the coefficients $e = \langle \mathbf{x}_{uu}, N\rangle$, $f = \langle \mathbf{x}_{uv}, N\rangle$, $g = \langle \mathbf{x}_{vv}, N\rangle$ pair with the metric coefficients $E, F, G$ to yield $K = (eg-f^2)/(EG-F^2)$ and $H = (eG - 2fF + gE)/(2(EG-F^2))$. The sign of $K = \det(dN_p)$ classifies every surface point into four distinct geometric regimes.

Point Type Curvature Condition Asymptotic Directions Local Shape & Canonical Example
Elliptic $K > 0$ None ($k_1, k_2$ same sign) Surface stays on one side of $T_p S$ (Sphere, outer Torus)
Hyperbolic $K < 0$ Two distinct lines Saddle crossing $T_p S$ (Helicoid, Catenoid, inner Torus, Enneper)
Parabolic $K = 0,\; A_p \ne 0$ One line ($k_2=0$ or $k_1=0$) Bends along one principal axis only (Cylinder, Torus crown/keel)
Planar $A_p = 0$ ($k_1=k_2=0$) Every direction Flat up to second order (Plane; isolated points on monkey saddles)

Section IV · Vector Fields, Trajectories & Topological Obstructions

You cannot comb a sphere flat, yet a torus admits seamless flow everywhere

A differentiable vector field on $V \subset S$ assigns a tangent vector $w(p) = a(u,v)\mathbf{x}_u + b(u,v)\mathbf{x}_v \in T_pS$ to each point, with integral trajectories solving $\alpha'(t) = w(\alpha(t))$. As the cylinder field $w = \mathbf{x}_u$ shows, constant coordinate coefficients $(1,0)$ still rotate in $\mathbb{R}^3$—foreshadowing Chapter 4’s covariant derivative.

Globally, topology governs where $w$ must vanish. By the Poincaré–Hopf / Hairy Ball Theorem, the sum of singularity indices equals the Euler characteristic $\chi(S)$. On $S^2$ ($\chi=2$), every continuous tangent field must have at least one zero; on a torus ($\chi=0$) or cylinder, tangent flow can circulate smoothly with zero singularities.

Section V · Ruled Surfaces, Minimal Surfaces & Bonnet Isometry

Straight rulings enforce $K \le 0$, while minimal surfaces bend into one another without stretching

A ruled surface $\mathbf{x}(t,v) = \alpha(t) + v\,w(t)$ ($|w|=1$) is swept by straight lines. Because $\mathbf{x}_{vv} = 0$, the coefficient $g$ vanishes identically, forcing $K = -f^2/(EG-F^2) \le 0$ everywhere—proving a sphere ($K>0$) can never contain an open ruled patch. When the triple product $(w, w', \alpha') = 0$, $f=0$ and $K=0$: the surface is developable. Along non-cylindrical ruled surfaces, the line of striction ($\langle \alpha'(t), w'(t)\rangle = 0$) traces the narrowest throat where neighboring rulings pass closest.

By contrast, a surface is minimal when its mean curvature vanishes ($H = 0$, so $k_2 = -k_1$ and $K = -k_1^2 \le 0$). The first variation of area under normal perturbations $\mathbf{x} + t h N$ is $A'(0) = -2\iint h H \sqrt{EG-F^2}\,du\,dv$, meaning $H \equiv 0$ characterizes critical points of area. Remarkably, the catenoid and helicoid are joined by a continuous 1-parameter family of minimal surfaces that preserves every intrinsic length and angle ($I_\theta \equiv I_0$).
